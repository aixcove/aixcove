  <h2>本周发生了什么</h2>
  <p>4月10日周四，缅因州立法机构将LD 2082法案送交州长办公室。该法案禁止在心理健康治疗中临床使用人工智能。它仍允许AI处理预约、账单和其他行政任务，但一旦聊天机器人开始扮演治疗师角色，即属违规。立法机构计划于4月15日休会，因此议员们紧急推动该法案通过。</p>
  <p>密苏里州也在朝同一方向推进。HB 2372是一项综合医疗法案，包含治疗聊天机器人禁令，已于4月2日在众议院全体通过，目前交由参议院家庭、老年人及健康委员会审议。该法案比缅因州的更进一步：涵盖治疗服务、心理治疗服务和心理健康诊断。首次违规罚款1万美元，由州检察长执行。</p>
  <p>这两个州并非凭空出现。俄勒冈州和爱达荷州在4月第一周均签署了与聊天机器人相关的法案。田纳西州州长比尔·李大约在同一时间签署了一项AI医疗法案。田纳西州立法机构还通过了另一项人格法案SB 837，规定人工智能不得被视为州法律下的“人”“生命”或“自然人”。参议院投票结果为26比6，众议院为93比2。这种跨党派票数差距在科技相关议题上并不常见。</p>
  <h2>触发整治的市场</h2>
  <p>这些立法都不是凭空产生的。心理健康聊天机器人市场预计到2033年将达到20.3亿美元，年增长率6.2%，北美占全球份额的42%。Woebot Health、Wysa和Youper等公司已围绕AI驱动的治疗工具建立了实际业务。Wysa甚至获得了FDA突破性设备认定，这一认定通常保留给解决未满足需求的医疗设备。</p>
  <p>问题在于规模扩张缺乏监管。这些产品开始触达弱势人群，处于活跃心理健康危机中的人，而监管框架却保持沉默。多款聊天机器人产品在临床或准临床环境中部署，却没有适用于人类治疗师的同等许可和监督要求。产品可用性与监管护栏之间的差距，正是这些法案试图填补的。</p>
  <h2>为什么各州比华盛顿行动更快</h2><p>联邦政府一边在各部门加速推进AI应用，一边为AI权限的边界打官司。各州的问题则更具体：聊天机器人能不能给心理危机患者做治疗？</p>
  <p>州议会的答案很明确：不能。佐治亚州的SB 540法案，涉及聊天机器人披露和儿童安全，上周会期结束后正等待州长签署。内布拉斯加州把AI聊天机器人安全条款LB 1185塞进了一项农业数据隐私法案，合并后的文本已进入最后宣读程序，州议会4月17日休会前将表决。伊利诺伊州的AI法案多到参议院专门成立了一个委员会来梳理。阿拉巴马州会期4月16日结束，AI相关法案也还在推进中。</p>
  <h2>临床与行政的界线</h2>
  <p>值得留意的是，缅因州和密苏里州的法案划出了同一条界线：AI可以用于医疗行政工作，禁止用于临床治疗。这不是一刀切反对AI，而是划定一个具体边界。</p>
  <p>这条边界之所以重要，是因为它给其他州提供了可复制的模板。立法者没有试图监管医疗领域的所有AI——那会既混乱又难以执行——而是瞄准了一个狭窄但情感上容易引起共鸣的类别：让机器去安抚一个有自杀倾向的青少年。反对监管这件事很难站住脚。</p>
  <p>但“行政”和“临床”的界线很快就会模糊。给患者分诊算不算诊断？推荐应对策略算不算治疗？聊天机器人问一句“你今天感觉怎么样”是否越过了临床边界？这些问题最终会闹到法院。</p>
  <h2>接下来会发生什么</h2>
  <p>到4月底，可能有五六个州会正式立法禁止AI心理治疗。各州法规拼凑出的监管版图，聊天机器人公司必须逐一应对，同时也会给国会施加压力，促使他们在AI监管上有所行动。</p><p>田纳西州的“人格法案”是这里最值得关注的动向。将AI在法律上定义为与人类截然不同的实体，听起来理所当然，但以93比2和26比6的票数将其写入法规，为其他州树立了先例。预计类似的人格法案会迅速蔓延。</p>
  <p>对于正在开发这些产品的公司来说，无监管部署的窗口刚刚关闭。Woebot、Wysa和Youper现在面临六个月前不存在的实际合规负担。那些依靠直接面向消费者的心理健康工具、却没有临床许可的增长策略的公司，需要寻找新出路。20亿美元的市场预测没有考虑到州级禁令，这本该被考虑进去。</p>

<h2>What Happened This Week</h2>
<p>On Thursday, April 10, Maine&#8217;s legislature sent LD 2082 to the governor&#8217;s desk. The bill prohibits the clinical use of artificial intelligence in mental health therapy. It still allows AI to handle scheduling, billing, and other administrative tasks, but the moment a chatbot starts acting as a therapist, that&#8217;s a violation. The legislature is scheduled to adjourn April 15, so lawmakers pushed this through with urgency.</p>
<p>Missouri is moving in the same direction. HB 2372, an omnibus healthcare bill that includes a therapy chatbot ban, passed the full House on April 2 and now sits with the Senate Committee on Families, Seniors and Health. This one goes further than Maine&#8217;s bill: it covers therapy services, psychotherapy services, and mental health diagnosis. First violation carries a $10,000 penalty, enforced by the state Attorney General.</p>
<p>These two states did not appear out of nowhere. Oregon and Idaho both signed chatbot-related bills into law during the first week of April. Tennessee governor Bill Lee signed an AI healthcare bill around the same time. The Tennessee legislature also passed a separate personhood bill, SB 837, defining that artificial intelligence cannot be considered a &#8220;person,&#8221; &#8220;life,&#8221; or &#8220;natural person&#8221; under state law. The Senate vote was 26-6. The House vote was 93-2. That kind of bipartisan margin is unusual for anything tech-related.</p>
<h2>The Market That Triggered the Crackdown</h2>
<p>None of this legislation exists in a vacuum. The mental health chatbot market is projected to hit $2.03 billion by 2033, growing at a 6.2% annual rate, with North America holding 42% of the global share. Companies like Woebot Health, Wysa, and Youper have built real businesses around AI-driven therapy tools. Wysa even received FDA Breakthrough Device Designation, which is normally reserved for medical devices that address unmet needs.</p>
<p>The problem is scale without oversight. These products started reaching vulnerable people, people in active mental health crises, while regulatory frameworks stayed silent. Several chatbot products were deployed in clinical or quasi-clinical settings without the same licensing and oversight requirements that apply to human therapists. That gap between product availability and regulatory guardrails is exactly what these bills are trying to close.</p>
<h2>Why States Are Moving Faster Than Washington</h2>
<p>The federal government is simultaneously accelerating AI adoption across agencies and fighting legal battles over where AI authority begins and ends. Meanwhile, states have a much narrower question to answer: should a chatbot be allowed to treat a person experiencing a mental health crisis?</p>
<p>The answer emerging from state legislatures is a clear no. Georgia&#8217;s SB 540, a chatbot disclosure and child safety bill, awaits the governor&#8217;s signature after the session ended last Monday. Nebraska attached an AI chatbot safety measure, LB 1185, to an agricultural data privacy bill, of all things, and the combined package has been placed on final reading before the legislature adjourns April 17.</p>
<p>Illinois has so many AI bills floating around that the Senate essentially formed a committee just to sort through them all. Alabama&#8217;s session wraps April 16, and AI measures are still in play there too.</p>
<h2>The Line Between Clinical and Administrative</h2>
<p>Here is the part worth paying attention to. Both the Maine and Missouri bills draw the same distinction: AI is fine for administrative healthcare tasks. AI is banned from clinical therapeutic work. This is not a blanket anti-AI stance. It is a specific boundary.</p>
<p>That boundary matters because it sets a template other states can copy. Instead of trying to regulate all AI in healthcare, which would be messy and probably unworkable, legislators are targeting a narrow, emotionally resonant category: a machine talking a suicidal teenager through their feelings. Hard to argue against regulating that.</p>
<p>But the line between &#8220;administrative&#8221; and &#8220;clinical&#8221; gets blurry fast. Does triaging a patient&#8217;s symptoms count as diagnosis? Does suggesting coping strategies count as therapy? Does a chatbot that asks &#8220;how are you feeling today?&#8221; cross into clinical territory? These are the questions that will end up in court.</p>
<h2>What Comes Next</h2>
<p>By the end of April, five or six states could have AI therapy bans on their books. That creates a patchwork of state regulations that chatbot companies will have to navigate, and it pressures Congress to act on something, anything, related to AI oversight.</p>
<p>The Tennessee personhood bill is the sleeper here. Defining AI as legally distinct from human beings sounds obvious, but getting that definition codified into statute with 93-2 and 26-6 margins gives other states a precedent. Expect to see similar personhood bills spread quickly.</p>
<p>For the companies building these products, the window for unregulated deployment just closed. Woebot, Wysa, and Youper now face a real compliance burden that did not exist six months ago. Companies that built growth strategies on direct-to-consumer mental health tools without clinical licensing are going to need new strategies. The $2 billion market projection did not account for state-level bans. It should have.</p>
