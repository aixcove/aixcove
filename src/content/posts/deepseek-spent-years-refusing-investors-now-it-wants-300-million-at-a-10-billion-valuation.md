  <h2>DeepSeek多年拒绝投资者，如今却要融资3亿美元，估值100亿美元</h2>
  <p>这家让华尔街在一个周末内市值蒸发约1万亿美元的中国AI初创公司，现在开始伸手要钱了。据The Information 2026年4月17日报道，总部位于杭州、推出R1推理模型的DeepSeek，正洽谈至少3亿美元的融资，估值超过100亿美元。消息援引四位知情人士的话。</p>
  <p>如果估值成立，DeepSeek将成为全球估值最高的私营AI公司之一。对一个直到本周都没拿过外部投资者一分钱的企业来说，这成绩不赖。</p>
  <h3>不情愿的初创公司</h3>
  <p>DeepSeek的出身在AI圈里算少见。公司脱胎于量化对冲基金幻方资本，该基金2025年回报率据报约56.6%。这笔收益给了创始人梁文锋足够的资金底气，让他能建一个AI研究实验室，而不必去讨好风投。多年来，他拒绝外部资金，担心投资者的压力会稀释公司以研究为先的文化，或者让这家有全球野心的中国AI公司本已微妙的地缘政治处境更复杂。</p>
  <p>当DeepSeek还只是个细分研究项目时，这套做法行得通。然后R1来了。</p>
  <h3>打破市场假设的模型</h3>
  <p>2025年初DeepSeek发布R1时，AI界迅速侧目。在多项基准测试中，该模型匹敌或接近OpenAI和Anthropic的顶级系统。真正让人瞪眼的是成本：R1据报训练费用在560万到600万美元之间，用的是英伟达H800芯片——这是美国制裁下中国企业不得不绕道使用的受限出口硬件。</p>
  <p>这个数字在“打造前沿AI模型需要数亿甚至数十亿美元算力投入”的主流假设下炸开一个洞。投资者慌了，芯片股下跌。一个被广泛引用的估算称，R1发布后几天内，AI相关公司市值蒸发了约1万亿美元。</p>
  <p>一个成立刚两年的初创公司，居然吓住了地球上资金最密集的科技行业。信号似乎很明确：想在前沿竞争，未必非得烧掉成堆的现金。</p><h3>为什么现在这笔钱很重要</h3>
  <p>但事实证明，你确实需要。DeepSeek的工程团队以用更少资源做更多事而闻名。KV缓存压缩和选择性激活等技术帮助在受限硬件上榨出惊人性能。公司开源了模型，并将API推理定价大幅低于竞争对手，吸引了大批用户。</p>
  <p>这股用户潮成了问题。故障越来越频繁，基础设施成本不断攀升。大规模运行AI服务，即使构建得再高效，需求激增时也会迅速烧钱。DeepSeek目前的算力根本跟不上使用量，而扩容需要硬件、数据中心容量，以及幻方量化单靠资产负债表可能撑不起的工程人力。</p>
  <p>矛盾是实实在在的。DeepSeek证明了小团队加上聪明的工程能力，可以在模型质量上匹敌大预算实验室。但每天服务数百万次API调用、保持服务在线、训练下一代模型？这需要另一个量级的资本。</p>
  <h3>谁写这张支票比支票金额更重要</h3>
  <p>100亿美元的估值引人注目，但投资者的身份可能更有意思。DeepSeek几乎肯定瞄准的是中国国内投资者。美国风投公司面临监管压力和国家安全限制，投资中国AI公司在政治上很敏感，甚至可能违法。即使有想入局的机构，也会受到华盛顿和北京的双重审视。</p>
  <p>这个限制大大缩小了选择范围。阿里巴巴、腾讯、百度等中国科技巨头都有自己的AI部门，可能把DeepSeek视为战略收购目标或竞争威胁。主权财富基金和国家背景的机构可能参与，但这会引发对DeepSeek独立性的质疑。公司一直以独立研究实验室的形象示人，拿中国政府的钱会迅速改变这个叙事。</p>
  <h3>这个估值说明了AI市场的什么</h3><p>对DeepSeek估值的猜测一直起伏不定。R1发布前，一些分析师认为其价值低至10亿美元，另一些人则提出超过200亿美元的数字。100亿美元这个中间值反映了两件事：该公司在全球AI对话中的巨大影响力，以及其相对精简的收入结构。DeepSeek不是OpenAI，后者年收入据报达100亿美元，并与财富500强企业签有企业合同。DeepSeek是一个研究实验室，附带一个仍在摸索商业模式的API业务。</p>
  <p>然而，这一估值传递了关于2026年年中AI投资周期所处位置的重要信号。即使在经历了一年的整合、关停（OpenAI的Sora视频应用将于4月26日停用，距今仅八天）以及对实验室何时能产生可持续利润的怀疑日益加剧之后，投资者仍愿意对模型构建公司下九位数的赌注。支票仍在开出，只是兑现时更有选择性。</p>
  <h3>更大的图景</h3>
  <p>DeepSeek的融资轮若顺利完成，将标志着这家以独立性为身份标识的公司的转折点。梁文锋愿意接受外部资本，表明即使是最自律、最具成本效益的AI实验室也正撞上与其挥霍无度的美国同行相同的墙。规模需要花钱。没有任何工程技巧能让运营一个全球AI服务变得便宜。</p>
  <p>这一点值得停下来想一想。这家证明了可以用600万美元训练前沿AI的初创公司，如今承认600万美元买不到足够的服务器机架。效率的故事是真的，但它不是全部。</p>
  <p>对整个AI行业而言，DeepSeek转向外部融资是一个信号：军备竞赛在加速，而非放缓。如果一家以少做多著称的公司现在需要外部资本，那对数十家效率较低、正在烧完A轮融资的初创公司意味着什么？领导者与其他所有人之间的算力差距可能正在扩大，而非缩小，尽管R1似乎曾承诺相反的结果。</p>

<h2>DeepSeek Spent Years Refusing Investors. Now It Wants $300 Million at a $10 Billion Valuation.</h2>
<p>The Chinese AI startup that made Wall Street lose roughly $1 trillion in market value over a single weekend is now asking for money. DeepSeek, the Hangzhou-based lab behind the R1 reasoning model, is in talks to raise at least $300 million at a valuation north of $10 billion, according to a report from The Information on April 17, 2026, citing four people familiar with the discussions.</p>
<p>If that valuation holds, it would make DeepSeek one of the most valuable private AI companies on the planet. Not bad for an outfit that, until this week, had never taken a dime from outside investors.</p>
<h3>The Reluctant Startup</h3>
<p>DeepSeek&#8217;s origin story is unusual in the AI space. The company was born out of High-Flyer Capital Management, a quantitative hedge fund that reportedly posted returns of about 56.6% in 2025. That performance gave founder Liang Wenfeng the financial runway to build an AI research lab without courting venture capital. For years, he refused external funding, worried that investor pressure would dilute the company&#8217;s research-first culture or complicate its already precarious geopolitical position as a Chinese AI company with global ambitions.</p>
<p>That stance worked well enough when DeepSeek was a niche research project. Then R1 happened.</p>
<h3>The Model That Broke the Market&#8217;s Assumptions</h3>
<p>When DeepSeek released R1 in early 2025, the AI world paid attention fast. The model matched or approached the performance of top-tier systems from OpenAI and Anthropic on several benchmarks. The jaw-dropper was the cost: R1 was reportedly trained for somewhere between $5.6 million and $6 million using Nvidia H800 chips, the export-restricted hardware that Chinese companies have had to work around due to U.S. sanctions.</p>
<p>That number detonated a bomb under the prevailing assumption that building frontier AI models required hundreds of millions, or billions, in compute spending. Investors panicked. Chip stocks slid. One widely cited estimate put the market reaction at roughly $1 trillion in erased value across AI-related companies in the days following R1&#8217;s release.</p>
<p>A startup barely two years old had managed to spook the most heavily funded technology sector on earth. The message seemed clear: maybe you don&#8217;t need to burn mountains of cash to compete at the frontier.</p>
<h3>Why the Money Matters Now</h3>
<p>Except, as it turns out, you kind of do. DeepSeek&#8217;s engineering team built its reputation on doing more with less. Techniques like KV cache compression and selective activation helped squeeze remarkable performance out of constrained hardware. The company open-sourced its models and priced its API inference dramatically below competitors, which attracted a flood of users.</p>
<p>That flood has become a problem. Outages have grown more frequent. Infrastructure costs are climbing. Running an AI service at scale, even an efficiently built one, burns cash fast when demand spikes. DeepSeek&#8217;s current compute capacity simply isn&#8217;t keeping up with usage, and expanding it requires hardware, data center capacity, and the kind of engineering headcount that High-Flyer&#8217;s balance sheet alone probably can&#8217;t sustain.</p>
<p>The tension is real. DeepSeek proved that a small team with clever engineering can match big-budget labs on model quality. But serving millions of API calls daily, keeping the service online, and training next-generation models? That requires capital at a different order of magnitude.</p>
<h3>Who Writes the Check Matters More Than the Check Size</h3>
<p>The $10 billion valuation is notable, but the identity of the investors may tell a more interesting story. DeepSeek is almost certainly targeting domestic Chinese investors. U.S. venture firms face regulatory pressure and national security restrictions that make investing in a Chinese AI company politically toxic, if not legally impossible. Even firms that might want in would face scrutiny from both Washington and Beijing.</p>
<p>This constraint narrows the field considerably. Chinese tech giants like Alibaba, Tencent, and Baidu have their own AI divisions and might see DeepSeek as either a strategic acquisition target or a competitive threat. Sovereign wealth funds and state-backed vehicles could participate, but that would raise questions about DeepSeek&#8217;s independence. The company has built its brand on being a scrappy, independent research lab. Taking money from the Chinese state would change that narrative in a hurry.</p>
<h3>What the Valuation Says About the AI Market</h3>
<p>Estimates of DeepSeek&#8217;s worth have swung wildly. Some analysts pegged it as low as $1 billion before the R1 release. Others floated figures above $20 billion. The $10 billion middle ground reflects two things: the company&#8217;s outsized influence on the global AI conversation, and its relatively lean revenue structure. DeepSeek is not OpenAI, with its reported $10 billion in annual revenue and enterprise contracts across Fortune 500 companies. It&#8217;s a research lab with an API business that&#8217;s still finding its commercial footing.</p>
<p>And yet the valuation signals something important about where the AI investment cycle sits in mid-2026. Investors are still willing to place nine-figure bets on model-building companies, even after a year of consolidation, shutdowns (OpenAI&#8217;s Sora video app is being discontinued on April 26, just eight days from now), and growing skepticism about when these labs will generate sustainable profits. The checks are still getting written. They&#8217;re just more selective about who cashes them.</p>
<h3>The Bigger Picture</h3>
<p>DeepSeek&#8217;s fundraising round, assuming it closes, would mark a turning point for a company that built its identity on independence. Liang Wenfeng&#8217;s willingness to accept outside capital suggests that even the most disciplined, cost-efficient AI labs are hitting the same wall as their free-spending American counterparts. Scale costs money. There is no engineering trick that makes running a global AI service cheap.</p>
<p>That&#8217;s worth sitting with for a moment. The startup that proved you could train frontier AI for $6 million is now admitting that $6 million doesn&#8217;t buy you enough server racks. The efficiency story was real. It just wasn&#8217;t the whole story.</p>
<p>For the broader AI industry, DeepSeek&#8217;s pivot to external funding is a signal that the arms race is accelerating, not slowing down. If a company famous for doing more with less now needs outside capital, what does that say about the dozens of less efficient startups burning through their Series A rounds? The compute gap between the leaders and everyone else may be widening, not closing, despite what R1 seemed to promise.</p>
